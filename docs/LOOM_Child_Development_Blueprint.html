Loom Child Studio

A child-centred extension to Threads of Hope that turns the two things every vulnerable child can already do — draw a picture and tell a story — into a developmental screening instrument, a ten-year Arts, Design & Culture curriculum, and a portfolio they own and walk out with at eighteen.

Right now the platform holds children as case files. Loom makes them authors. Same database, opposite relationship: the child stops being the record and becomes the one who writes it.

01

The pivot this project rests on

The repository already contains a working child-welfare system: Child, ChildEvent, an NGO console, donations, messaging. Everything in it treats the child in the third person. Nothing in it is addressed to the child. That single absence is the research opening and the product opportunity at the same time.

What exists today

the child as case record
  • Child stores trauma history, legal status, guardian details, rescue circumstances — a case file written by adults.
  • ChildEvent logs health_checkup, placement_change, incident — things that happen to a child.
  • No child account. No child login. The User.role enum has no child value.
  • Badge and Enrollment exist, but attach to User — so only women can earn or enrol.
  • Development is captured as free text (academicPerformance), never as measurable signal.
  • Nothing leaves with the child. At eighteen the record closes and the child exits with nothing in hand.

What Loom adds

the child as author
  • A child account with avatar + PIN login, guardian-consented, requiring no email, no phone, no literacy.
  • A Studio — a browser canvas and a story microphone — where the child makes work.
  • Every creation is scored on-device into developmental signals: motor control, spatial reasoning, narrative structure, expressive range.
  • Signals drive an adaptive quest ladder across four stages from age 8 to 18.
  • Flat or falling signals raise a quiet early-warning to a teacher — never to the child, never automatically escalated.
  • Ten years of work compound into The Golden Thread: a verified, exportable, child-owned creative portfolio and skills record.
The women's work is untouched. Training programmes, enrolments, progress tracking, certificates, donations and messaging all stay exactly as built. The women gain one new thing — the role of Atelier Guide — which puts their tailoring and craft skill to work on children's designs, and gives the platform a revenue loop it does not have today.
02

One drawing and one story, four outputs

This is the mechanism the whole proposal turns on. A child spends twelve minutes making something. From that single act the system produces four distinct things that normally require four separate systems, four budgets and a visiting psychologist. The child experiences none of it as a test.

The child makes

One drawing — on the canvas, or photographed from paper with the classroom phone.

One spoken story — 30 to 90 seconds, in Luganda, English or Kiswahili, about the thing they drew.

NO READING REQUIRED · NO INTERNET REQUIRED

→
Developmental signal

Fine-motor control, spatial reasoning, figure complexity, narrative causality, lexical range — scored on-device, trended over time.

The next lesson

The weakest and the strongest axis together pick the child's next quest on the Arts, Design & Culture ladder.

A safeguarding flag

A flatlined motor index or a narrative that turns isolating across several cycles raises a private alert to a named adult.

A portfolio entry

The work, the date, the brief and the growth it evidences are pinned permanently into the child's Golden Thread.

Why this is the defensible idea. Commercial school analytics need literate learners and a connection. Clinical screening tools need a trained psychologist and half an hour per child. Creative apps produce pretty work and no measurement. Loom takes the artefact children in these classrooms already produce every week — a drawing and a told story — and extracts assessment, curriculum, safeguarding and economic value from it at once, on a shared Android phone with the data switched off.
03

What makes it child-centred, concretely

"Child-centred" fails a rubric when it stays a value. Each principle below is paired with the specific product decision that enforces it — which is what an examiner, and later a user, can actually check.

Child agency and ownershipUNCRC Art. 12 · Hart's ladder

The child owns the account and the portfolio. They can mark a piece private, choose which work is shown publicly, and export everything when they leave. Guardians and teachers get scoped views, not ownership. Older children sit on the panel that writes new quest briefs.

The hundred languagesReggio Emilia

Drawing, modelling, pattern, performance and spoken story are first-class submissions — not enrichment after the "real" subjects. The canvas and the microphone are the primary input surfaces of the application.

Scaffolding in the zone of proximal developmentVygotsky

Quest difficulty is set from the child's own last three submissions, not from their age or grade. Every quest ships with a "do it with someone" variant that pairs the child with a mentor or an older peer.

Mastery, not age-gradingcompetency-based progression

A fourteen-year-old who lost four years of school starts at the stage their work places them in and moves at their own pace. Attainment states are not yet · emerging · secure · mastery. There is no failure state anywhere in the data model.

Invisible, formative assessmentassessment as learning

The assessment is the artwork. No timed tests, no exam room, no literacy requirement. A child who has never sat a written paper still generates a complete developmental profile by drawing their grandmother.

Trauma-informed by constructionstrengths-based practice

Strengths-first language, no red fail states, no public leaderboards. Safeguarding indicators are never shown to the child, never shown to peers, and never auto-escalate — they open a private triage card for a named, trained adult.

Culturally sustaining contentParis & Alim

Briefs are built from Baganda motif and barkcloth traditions, kitenge pattern systems, Luganda proverbs and folktales, and family oral history — not translated Western worksheets. Luganda is a first-class interface language beside English and Kiswahili, with recorded audio instruction on every screen for non-readers.

Autonomy, competence, relatednessself-determination theory

The child chooses between three open quests (autonomy), watches their own growth curve move (competence), and is paired with a guide and a peer circle (relatedness). These are the three retention levers, and they are explicit in the schema.

Universal design for learningCAST UDL 2.2

Icon-first navigation, audio instructions on every screen, no step that requires reading. Large touch targets, high-contrast mode, one-handed phone layout, full keyboard and screen-reader paths.

Learning Stories, not gradesCarr & Lee

Progress is a narrative record co-authored by the child, the teacher and the guardian against a longitudinal archive of real work — the opposite of a term-end number.

Peer teaching as progressionreciprocal learning

Reaching Stage 4 means teaching Stage 1. Mentoring hours are a tracked competency, not volunteering. It is how the cycle visibly breaks inside one cohort.

The child leaves holding somethingexit with assets

At eighteen the child exports a portfolio, a skills passport, a public portfolio URL and a mentor reference. The alternative — a care record that closes and an empty pair of hands — is the status quo this project exists to end.

04

The skills ladder, ages 8 to 18

Four stages across ten years, each with four strands. Everything here is practical: a skill a child can perform, a teacher can witness, and the system can evidence. The right-hand columns say what the software reads from the work and what the child can demonstrably do by the end of each stage.

STAGE 1 · AGES 8–10

Foundation

"I can make a mark that means something."

Draw & See

  • Line and pressure control — continuous line, varied weight, staying inside a boundary
  • Closed-shape formation — circles, squares, triangles drawn closed and even
  • Grid copying and scaling — reproducing a shape larger or smaller
  • Draw-a-person — the human figure, built up part by part over the year
  • Symmetry and pattern repetition — mirroring, and repeating a motif across a strip
  • Observational drawing — drawing a real object in front of them, not from memory
  • Colour mixing and naming in all three platform languages

Design & Make

  • Paper folding and net construction — flat sheet into a standing object
  • Card-loom weaving — the first real over-under, and the project's namesake
  • Clay and mud modelling — pinch, coil, join
  • Collage from scrap — composition from found material
  • Simple beadwork — threading to a stated colour sequence
  • Measuring by hand and by ruler — the first bridge into numeracy

Story & Culture

  • Retelling a folktale in sequence — beginning, middle, end, in order
  • The 60-second "my day" narration, recorded
  • Naming feelings through a character — safer than naming your own
  • Call-and-response song and rhythm — timing, listening, turn-taking
  • Describing your own drawing aloud — the core weekly ritual

Tools & Trade

  • Caring for materials — brushes, lids, sharing, returning
  • Using the tablet or phone canvas — draw, undo, save
  • Recording your own voice — press, speak, stop, listen back
  • Photographing paper work so it joins the digital thread

What the system reads

Stroke count and length · line tremor from curvature variance · shape closure rate · ink coverage and canvas use · bounding-box symmetry · figure-part count (a Draw-a-Person style index) · utterance length · unique word count · pause ratio · sequence markers in the retelling.

Exit standard

Draws a closed, recognisable human figure with six or more parts; repeats a motif accurately across a strip; records a 60-second told story with a clear beginning, middle and end; completes a woven card and a folded standing form unaided.

STAGE 2 · AGES 11–13

Craft

"I can make something that works."

Draw & See

  • Proportion and scale — the head-count method for figures
  • Light, shadow and form — making a flat circle read as a sphere
  • One-point perspective — a street, a room, a market stall
  • Character design sheets — one character, four angles, consistent
  • Six-panel storyboarding — the first real control of time and sequence
  • Hand lettering — letterform, spacing, a drawn headline

Design & Make

  • Textile pattern design — building a repeat from a kitenge or kanga motif system
  • Hand sewing and basic garment construction — the direct handover to the women's hub
  • Block and screen printing — cutting a block, pulling a clean print
  • Beadwork and jewellery to a brief — with a stated budget
  • Product sketching with dimensions — an idea another person could build
  • An upcycling brief — a sellable object from waste material
  • Costing a made object — materials, time, what it should sell for

Story & Culture

  • Writing and performing a short scripted scene
  • Interviewing an elder — oral history captured, consented and archived
  • Proverb interpretation — unpacking a saying into an image
  • Documentary photography — a five-frame photo essay on a phone
  • A three-minute audio story — recorded, trimmed, published to the class gallery

Tools & Trade

  • Digital drawing with layers, on a mouse or a touchscreen
  • Photographing work properly — light, square-on, cropped, colour-true
  • File naming, folders and export formats — the unglamorous skill that decides whether work survives
  • Giving and taking critique — two things that worked, one change
  • Working to a deadline and revising after feedback

What the system reads

Compositional complexity and depth cues · tonal range · motif repeat accuracy · iteration count per brief (how many versions before submission) · revision responsiveness after critique · narrative causality rather than mere sequence — because and so replacing and then · type-token ratio · brief-to-delivery completion rate.

Exit standard

Delivers a finished object against a written brief and a budget; produces a tileable textile repeat printed onto real cloth; sews a simple garment or bag to measurement; publishes a three-minute recorded story and a five-frame photo essay; gives structured critique to a peer.

STAGE 3 · AGES 14–16

Studio

"I can make it for someone other than myself."

Draw & See

  • An illustration series — consistent style across six or more pieces
  • Portraiture — likeness, and the ethics of drawing a real person
  • Large-format and mural work — scaling up, working in a team
  • Visual identity systems — one idea expressed across many surfaces

Design & Make

  • Graphic design fundamentals — hierarchy, grid, colour theory, type pairing
  • Logo and brand identity for a real local business or a classmate's venture
  • Poster, flyer and social-media asset design at correct dimensions
  • UI/UX basics — user flows, wireframes, clickable prototypes bridge to tech
  • Packaging and label design — including print-safe setup
  • Fashion flats and a tech pack — the drawing a tailor can cut from
  • Prototyping — card, cloth or clay model before committing material

Story & Culture

  • Scriptwriting — structure, conflict, resolution
  • A three-to-five minute short film, shot and cut on a phone
  • Spoken word and performance poetry before a live audience
  • Curating an exhibition — selection, sequence, wall text, invitation
  • Pitching your own work — standing up and explaining why it is good

Tools & Trade

  • Figma or Penpot — components, frames, prototypes
  • Inkscape, Krita and GIMP — free, offline-capable, no licence barrier
  • Phone videography and editing; audio editing in Audacity
  • HTML and CSS to publish a portfolio page — the first line of code, written because they need it
  • Colour profiles, bleed, DPI and print handover
  • Crediting, licensing and copyright — their rights and other people's
  • The client loop: brief → concept → revision → delivery → invoice

What the system reads

Audience-awareness in the work · stylistic consistency across a series · number of revision cycles survived without abandonment · brief-fidelity scored against the stated constraints · peer-critique quality given and received · time from brief to delivery · first evidence of a chosen specialism emerging.

Exit standard

Completes three briefs for real external clients; ships a brand identity with a usage sheet; builds and publishes a working portfolio page they wrote themselves; directs and edits a short film; hangs and explains work in a public exhibition.

STAGE 4 · AGES 17–18

Enterprise

"I can make a living, and I can teach it."

Specialise

  • Choose one track and go deep: illustration · textile & fashion · graphic & brand · digital product and UI · film & photography · performance & storytelling
  • A capstone body of work in that track — eight to twelve finished pieces
  • An artist or designer statement that survives being read aloud

Professional practice

  • Pricing creative work — day rate, project rate, licensing
  • Writing a quote, a proposal and an invoice
  • Deposits, contracts and scope — how not to be exploited
  • Client communication — confirming, chasing, saying no
  • Supplier and printer liaison; getting a real quote from a real vendor
  • A creative CV and a portfolio website they maintain themselves

Enterprise

  • Costing for profit — materials plus labour plus margin, honestly
  • Mobile money, bookkeeping and separating business from personal
  • Registering a micro-business and what it requires locally
  • Selling — market stall, commission, online shop, social presence
  • Producing at volume with the women's cooperative — their design, the hub's machines
  • Applying — to art schools, apprenticeships, agencies, grants and residencies

Lead & hand on

  • Mentoring Stage 1 children — logged hours, assessed as a competency
  • Running a community workshop end to end
  • Documenting and archiving community heritage for the platform's cultural collection
  • Co-writing the next cohort's quest briefs — the child becomes the curriculum author

What the system reads

A six-axis readiness index — craft, conceptual range, professional practice, enterprise literacy, cultural leadership, mentoring — plus completed external commissions, verified income earned, mentoring hours logged, and portfolio completeness against the exit specification.

Exit standard — what an eighteen-year-old walks out with

The Golden Thread: ten years of dated, verified work; a specialism capstone; three or more paid or exhibited commissions; a self-built public portfolio URL; a creative CV and skills passport; recorded mentoring hours; and a named mentor reference. In hand, in their own account, on the day they leave.

The Thread-to-Market loop

This is where the children's module and the women's module stop being two products in one repository and become one system. A child designs; the women's cooperative — already in the database, already trained in tailoring — produces it; a donor or a customer buys it; and the money splits between the child's education fund and the woman who made it. It is the mission statement rendered as a database transaction.

Design

A Stage 2–4 child submits a textile repeat, a print or a product design against a seasonal brief.

child
Select

An Atelier Guide — a woman from the existing training cohort — reviews, selects and costs the design with the child.

woman · ngo
Produce

The cooperative's sewing and craft hub manufactures a short run. Existing enrolment and skills records show who is qualified to make it.

woman
Sell

The piece lists in the Atelier shop. Donors and buyers purchase through the donation rails already in the platform.

donor · public
Split

Revenue divides on a transparent, recorded rule: the child's education fund, the maker's wage, and the hub's materials float.

system
Report

An impact report generates automatically — the drawing, the finished product, the maker, the child, the sum. Real provenance, not a stock photo.

ngo · donor
Why it belongs in the proposal. It converts the project's social claim into measurable outcomes an examiner can test: units produced, revenue split, education-fund balances, maker-hours paid. It also gives donors something no NGO dashboard offers — a purchase whose origin they can trace back to a specific child's drawing on a specific date.
The safeguard that must be built in. Children earn into a restricted education fund with guardian and NGO co-signature; children are never customer-facing; no child's face, full name or location appears on a public listing. Child-labour and child-protection compliance is a design requirement of this module, not an afterthought — and the proposal should say so in the scope section.
06

The full-stack build

The earlier mission note proposed a downloadable desktop application. For a full-stack web specialisation — and, more importantly, for the devices these classrooms actually have — the stronger artifact is an offline-first progressive web application. It installs to the home screen without a store, runs on the shared Android phone and the staffroom laptop from one codebase, updates without redistributing an installer, and extends the React and Express stack already in this repository. Choosing the PWA is itself an argued technical contribution, and should be defended as one in Chapter One — as should the two toolchain decisions below.

Stack decisions

Three choices were made against what the repository actually contains today, not against what its README claims. Each is defensible in Chapter One as an engineering decision rather than a preference.

LayerTodayFor LoomWhy
Build toolCreate React App (react-scripts 5)ViteCRA blocks all three PWA pillars: the service worker needs craco or an eject to reach webpack config, ONNX .wasm assets need manual copying, and workers are awkward. CRA is also deprecated. Vite handles all three natively.

Language, back endJavaScript, SequelizeStays JavaScriptSequelize with TypeScript costs more friction than it returns mid-project. Shared request and response types live in one shared/types folder both sides import.
DatabaseSupabase as a plain Postgres host via Sequelize — no supabase-js, no RLSUnchanged, plus Supabase StorageThe connection works; leave it. Storage holds CreativeWork media — drawings, audio, photographed paper work — replacing the AWS S3 plan and removing one account to manage.
Client → data pathBrowser → Express → PostgresUnchanged. No direct supabase-js from the browserTwo reasons, both load-bearing: Supabase offers no offline write path, so the IndexedDB outbox is doing that work anyway; and going direct would make RLS policies the entire security boundary for trauma histories and safeguarding alerts.
The migration is small — do it in Phase 1. The front end is about 30 files. CRA to Vite is moving index.html to the root, swapping process.env.REACT_APP_* for import.meta.env.VITE_*, and fixing imports. One to two days, and it buys TypeScript and the whole PWA toolchain in a single decision instead of three. Doing it before the Studio is written costs far less than retrofitting it afterwards.
Two Supabase constraints to plan for. Free-tier projects pause after roughly a week of inactivity, which is a real risk across a 12-week field deployment — budget for the paid tier or build a keep-alive. And check the current region list before provisioning: round-trip latency from East Africa is a measurable part of sync time, so pick the closest available region and record the choice in your methodology.

Offline-first layer

  • Service worker via vite-plugin-pwa (Workbox under the hood) — app shell precache, stale-while-revalidate for media, model files cached on first run. One plugin, no eject.
  • IndexedDB via Dexie — works, signals, quests and the outbox persist locally; a full session completes with the radio off
  • Background Sync — the outbox drains when connectivity returns; every operation carries an idempotency key
  • Conflict policy — server-authoritative for competency state, last-write-wins for drafts, append-only for works and signals, so nothing a child made can be overwritten
  • Installable — manifest.json with maskable icons and an install prompt; no app store, no data bundle to download

On-device scoring

  • Explainable features first — stroke count, path length, curvature variance, closure rate, ink coverage, symmetry, bounding-box ratio, computed in plain JavaScript. These work with no model at all and give the project a defensible floor.
  • Quantised vision model — a MobileNet-class backbone with regression heads, run through ONNX Runtime Web (WASM + SIMD) in a Web Worker so the canvas never stutters. Vite resolves the .wasm assets and the module worker without config surgery.
  • Audio features — Web Audio AnalyserNode for speech rate, pause ratio, phonation-time ratio and pitch variance. Language-independent, so they work for Luganda from day one. See the tiered pipeline below.
  • Budget — under three seconds per submission on a 2 GB Android, models under 15 MB total
  • Human-in-the-loop — every signal is advisory, every alert is confirmed by a trained adult, and no score is ever shown to a child as a grade

The Luganda audio pipeline

Both deployment sites are in Kampala, so Luganda is the language most of these children actually tell stories in. Rather than treat that as a limitation, the pipeline is tiered so that the parts which need no transcription work immediately, and only the one index that genuinely needs words depends on a speech model.

TierWhat it producesNeeds transcription?Runs where
1Acoustic — speech rate, pause ratio and distribution, phonation-time ratio, pitch range, energy dynamics → fluency and expressive rangeNo. Language-independentOn-device, immediately, for any language a child speaks
2Keyword spotting over a closed set of roughly 50–100 Luganda narrative connectives — kuba, awo, olwo, naye — detecting causal and temporal markers → narrative structureNo open-vocabulary ASR. A small keyword-spotting modelOn-device, inside the 15 MB model budget
3Full transcription → lexical diversity and vocabulary growthYesServer-side, deferred until sync. Transcription is never needed in real time, so the offline guarantee is untouched

Tier 2 is the design decision worth defending. The system does not need a verbatim transcript — it needs to know whether causal and temporal connectives appear, because that is precisely the Stage 2 signal where narrative causality replaces mere sequence. Closed-vocabulary keyword spotting is far cheaper than open-vocabulary recognition and fits on the phone.

Luganda is better resourced than most African languages — and the work is in Kampala

  • Makerere Radio Speech Corpus — 155 hours of Luganda radio speech, the first publicly available radio dataset in sub-Saharan Africa, with baseline ASR results
  • Sunbird AI SALT — 25,000 parallel sentences across English, Luganda, Lugbara, Acholi, Runyankole, Ateso and Kiswahili, roughly 5,000 recorded for both ASR and TTS; open source
  • asr-whisper-large-v3-salt — Whisper Large v3 adapted to Luganda and ten other Ugandan and regional languages, which matters because stock Whisper does not cover Luganda at all
  • wav2vec2-luganda — XLSR-53 fine-tuned on Common Voice Luganda, available now
  • Common Voice Luganda, built through Makerere AI Lab's crowdsourcing push, plus the community models from the Mozilla–Zindi Luganda ASR challenge

Makerere AI Lab and Sunbird AI are both in Kampala, as are both deployment sites. A named local partnership on the language model is realistic, and reads in the proposal as institutional grounding rather than dependence on a foreign API. Check the current state of each before writing up — both ship regularly.

Three limitations to state honestly in Chapter One, because an examiner will find them. First, every Luganda corpus listed above is adult speech — radio broadcasts, read sentences, studio recordings. Child spontaneous speech is higher-pitched, more disfluent and differently worded, and recognition trained on adults degrades badly on eight-year-olds; this is why Tier 3 is scoped as deferred and assisted rather than assumed. Second, a drawing-based developmental index built on Western norming samples carries real bias risk, so the project must include a local norming exercise and a documented bias audit across gender, age and disability status, and must never present a score as a clinical diagnosis. Third, all scoring is advisory: the strongest comparison in this design is a child against their own trajectory, which sidesteps cross-language norming entirely.
The gap is also the contribution. The teacher-confirmation step produces gold-labelled Luganda child speech — a resource that does not appear to exist. Across a 12-week deployment with 120 children submitting weekly, that is a corpus of real size. Released consented and anonymised, it turns the project's sharpest stated limitation into a citable research output alongside the software, and gives the write-up a second contribution to claim.

1.2 Problem statement

The rubric wants the two closest existing solutions named, cited, and shown to fall short. Yours are already well chosen — here they are sharpened, with a third worth a sentence.

closest solution 1

Commercial school analytics — Zeraki Analytics, QuickSchools and similar

Subscription-priced, cloud-dependent, and built around secondary-school examination preparation. They digitise binary attendance and numeric exam scores. Where they fall short: they require a literate learner, a reliable connection and a recurring fee; they capture outcome, never development; and they have no representation at all for a child who cannot yet read.

closest solution 2

Paper developmental instruments — ASQ-3, Goodenough-Harris / Naglieri Draw-A-Person, Denver-II, MDAT

Clinically validated and genuinely well designed. Where they fall short: twenty to thirty minutes of one-to-one administration by a trained assessor, manual scoring, and a paper record that never becomes a trend. At the pupil–teacher ratios of a community school they are not merely difficult — the literature shows they are abandoned.

worth one sentence

Consumer creative and learning apps

Cloud-first, English-first, and designed for a child with their own device and a parent's account. They produce output but no measurement, no teacher view, no safeguarding path and no portability.

The gap this project addresses

No system exists that is offline-first, multimodal across visual and oral expression, longitudinal from age 8 to 18, and child-owned — one that converts the creative work these classrooms already produce into developmental signal, an adaptive curriculum, a safeguarding early warning and a portable record, on the low-cost devices these schools actually have.

1.3 Main objective

To design, develop and evaluate Loom, an offline-first progressive web application extending the Threads of Hope platform, which uses children's drawings, designs and recorded oral stories as both a continuous developmental screening instrument and an adaptive Arts, Design & Culture curriculum for learners aged 8 to 18 — producing, by age 18, a verified and exportable creative portfolio the child owns.

1.6 Significance and justification

The assignment asks for a maximum of three short paragraphs. These are drafted to that constraint.

Successful implementation puts developmental screening into schools that have never had it. A teacher managing forty children gains an evidence trail on each one, built from work those children were producing anyway, and learns about a motor delay or a widening withdrawal in weeks rather than at the point of grade failure.

For the child, ten years of creative work stop evaporating and start compounding. At eighteen they hold a verified portfolio, a named specialism, real commissions and a mentor reference — tradeable assets in a creative economy that is one of the few growth sectors genuinely open to them.

For the wider field, the project demonstrates that multimodal developmental assessment can run entirely on-device, in a browser, offline, on hardware these schools already own — removing the subscription, the connection and the visiting specialist that currently make such screening a privilege of well-resourced systems.
